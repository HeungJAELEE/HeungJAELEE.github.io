Project background and objective

Product development and equipment SAT had been completed, but the actual new product did not match the inherited machine and inspection conditions. The line could not run continuously from Start to the final operation, and operators relied on unit-by-unit manual operation and temporary recipes, creating variation in welding, inspection, and final geometry.

Changes in product geometry, reflectivity, and turn count were also missing from the laser, AI-vision, and X-ray settings. After the previous development owners left, the engineering team re-established equipment geometry and process conditions using actual cells and separated setting-related problems from equipment that required mechanical or software changes.

The objective was to restore geometry and process conditions on three laser-welding machines, rebuild X-ray and AI-vision inspection reliability, and establish controlled manual work and inspection standards for equipment that could not be redesigned immediately. This created a practical limited-production setup capable of a continuous full-process run.

Equipment inspection sequence

  1. Reviewed manual steps, recurring alarms, and product abnormalities with manufacturing operators.
  2. Restated each machine’s process function and the product characteristic it had to control.
  3. Restored jig contact, product reference position, alignment, and machine geometry on the laser equipment.
  4. Reset welding and inspection conditions only after the machines repeated their intended motion at the correct position.
  5. Correlated sectioning, tensile tests, cell teardown, X-ray, and AI-vision results through the same product flow.
  6. Separated adjustments that could be completed through settings from mechanical and software changes requiring redesign.
  7. Retested the complete process with two 100-cell lots and then ran a final 50 cells continuously from the first to the last operation.

Critical equipment stabilization and process optimization

Collector Laser Welding

This machine joins the positive and negative uncoated edges of the jelly roll to the collector plates, forming the cell’s internal current path. At the start, the machine could not enter Auto Cycle reliably, and joined-layer and effective-area variation remained high even during manual operation.

Joining-variation cause disposition

4M Candidate Evidence Decision Action
Man Operator differences in setting order and jig checks Compared work sequence on the same machine and samples Recurrence-control factor Standardize one setting and jig-check sequence
Machine Machine geometry, collector alignment, and poor jig contact Matched joining variation with position and contact condition Primary cause Restore geometry, alignment, and contact before recipe changes
Material New jelly-roll-to-collector joining geometry Compared inherited settings with the actual joint geometry Mandatory setting input Reflect in weld-condition definition
Method Temporary recipes built around an abnormal position Compared joint results before and after geometry restoration Follow-on adjustment axis Reset conditions after restoring machine geometry
Measurement Cross-section, microscopy, cell teardown, and IR Linked joining variation and downstream electrical result Application evidence Retest through section, teardown, and IR

Machine geometry and the collector reference position were restored first, followed by jig-contact adjustment and weld-condition optimization. Cross-sections, microscopy, and teardown were used to apply a condition that reduced joining variation while maintaining the required IR level.

Taping & Can Insert / Can Collector Welding

After the jelly roll is inserted into the can, this process joins the positive collector to the positive can terminal. The initial effective weld area, penetration depth, tensile strength, and appearance were unstable.

Penetration and tensile instability disposition

4M Candidate Evidence Decision Action
Man No operator-handling difference was directly linked to the representative failure within the checked scope Compared section and tensile samples under the same work sequence Not selected as a primary cause Retain the standard work sequence
Machine Jig and joint alignment and the product reference position Compared effective weld area and penetration with machine position Primary cause Realign machine geometry and reference position
Material Collector-to-can joining geometry Compared joint geometry with effective-weld results Mandatory setting input Reflect in the effective-weld requirement
Method Effective weld area and weld conditions Compared section/tensile results after geometry restoration Core adjustment axis Reset effective weld area and weld conditions
Measurement Five etched cross-sections and five tensile/fracture samples Cross-checked penetration, tensile strength, and fracture form Application evidence Validate the condition with section and tensile samples

Machine geometry and the product reference position were corrected before the effective weld area and process conditions were reset. Five sectioned samples and five tensile samples were evaluated for penetration, tensile strength, and fracture form, and the revised condition was transferred to production.

Beading & Collector Welding

This machine joins the negative collector to the can beading section and completes the negative current path. Collector position and weld alignment did not coincide, producing repeated position errors and manual intervention.

Position-error cause disposition

4M Candidate Evidence Decision Action
Man Manual intervention repeated after position errors Position errors repeated regardless of operator change Not selected as the primary cause Retain one recovery sequence
Machine Vision-head angle and collector reference position Compared detected/weld positions with physical angle Primary cause Realign the vision head and collector reference
Material Can-beading and collector geometry Compared product geometry with detection and weld positions Mandatory setting input Reflect in detection angle and weld position
Method Detection angle did not match the actual weld position Compared detection and weld results by angle Core adjustment axis Reset detection and welding under one reference
Measurement Five initial samples and two lots for detection, weld result, and Auto Cycle Checked position errors and motion by sample and lot Application evidence Retest initial samples and then two lots

The detectable collector angle and weld position were realigned. Position and motion were checked with five initial samples and then retested across two lots, enabling automatic operation and position inspection.

Common verification items

Laser conditions were reviewed not only for external joint quality but also for the risk of beam entry into the jelly roll and separator. Collector Laser and Beading Welding used available teardown samples to check internal damage; Can Collector Welding used section, tensile, and fracture results as its primary joint criteria.

Automatic versus visual inspection decision

4M Candidate Evidence Decision Action
Man Operator variation in visual inspection and MES entry Compared V1/V2/V5 inspection and entry records Recurrence-control factor Standardize inspection, disposition, and MES entry
Machine Vision-head angle, product reference position, illumination, and focus Compared recognition by inspection location and physical setup V4 could be corrected mechanically Adjust the V4 head and fix the reference setup

V3 and V4 retained automatic inspection because reliable disposition could be achieved with the existing model and mechanical adjustment. V1, V2, and V5 were converted to standardized visual inspection and manual MES entry, controlling both inspection omission and production bottlenecks.

A product made with the established pattern was designated as the reference sample. Product position, illumination, focus, inspection regions, and limit-sample handling were standardized to reduce operator-to-operator disposition differences.

X-ray inspection

X-ray inspection checks jelly-roll turn count, positive/negative electrode overlap, and overhang. The product structure had changed, but the original Count and ROI settings remained, so the inspection output no longer matched the actual internal geometry.

Count and ROI reset decision

4M Candidate Evidence Decision Action
Man Setting execution and final product disposition were split across owners Retested the team member’s settings on the same samples Common interpretation required Align trial direction, retest, and final application decision
Machine Inspection region versus product reference position Compared internal geometry with ROI location Primary adjustment axis Realign product structure inside the effective ROI
Material New turn count and internal geometry Compared inherited product with teardown and sections Mandatory change axis Reflect in Count and ROI inputs
Method Inherited Count and ROI settings X-ray did not match teardown and section results Primary cause Reset Count and ROI for the new structure
Measurement X-ray, cell teardown, cross-section, and microscopy Correlated turn count, overlap, and overhang Automatic-inspection evidence Apply after same-sample correlation

An engineering team member performed Count/ROI setting, sectioning, and microscopy. I defined the trial direction, judged whether the product was actually abnormal, reviewed the retest, and made the final application recommendation. The correlated results were used to reset automatic inspection for turn count, electrode overlap, and overhang.

Other production-readiness changes

Automatic-ready conditions

Operators initially reset product sensing, axis Home, and Ready conditions one by one before automatic operation. Maintenance revised the PLC sequence and interlocks, while I defined the Ready requirements from the equipment-acceptance basis and process purpose and provided technical support.

Ready-entry cause disposition

4M Candidate Evidence Decision Action
Man Sensor, Home, and Ready checks and resets differed by operator Compared trained-operator preparation and repeatability Recurrence-control factor Standardize one preparation sequence
Machine Product sensors, axis Home, Ready, PLC sequence, and interlocks Compared permissive status with Auto entry Primary cause Rebuild automatic-ready entry while retaining safety functions
Material No material or product characteristic was directly linked to Ready establishment Retested sensor, Home, and sequence conditions on the same product Not selected as a primary cause Retain the existing input criteria
Method Preparation depended on individual resets Compared individual reset with permissive-based preparation Primary improvement target Enter Ready after all permissives are satisfied
Measurement Repeatability of the same preparation sequence in manufacturing trials Repeated trials by trained operators Application evidence Confirm the automatic-ready procedure

Operator, door, and laser safety remained active. Once product sensor permissives and axis-position conditions were satisfied, the machine entered the automatic-ready state. Manufacturing trials confirmed that trained operators could prepare the equipment through one consistent sequence.

Measurement and inspection standardization

I directly wrote or revised the criteria for joined layers, effective weld area, penetration depth, fracture form, AI-vision illumination/focus/inspection regions, the quality measurement standard, P-FMEA, and reference-sample management.

Vision setting sheets, limit samples, X-ray Count/ROI, manufacturing check sheets, QCP, manual MES entry, and visual-inspection training materials prepared by the engineering team were reviewed against the actual production conditions and aligned across departments.

Equipment design and software gaps with production controls

Machines that could not be resolved through setting or recipe changes were separated into production controls and permanent improvement actions. Temporary work was standardized to protect production, while mechanical and software changes were transferred through punch lists and RFQ actions.

E/L Filling

E/L Filling meters electrolyte into the cell and maintains the seal during filling. Leakage, insufficient and variable fill quantity, and contamination repeatedly occurred at the O-ring connection between the hopper and chamber.

Leakage and fill-variation disposition

4M Candidate Evidence Decision Action
Man Spare replacement and leak/fill checks depended on operator judgment Compared replacement and inspection order by operator Recurrence-control factor Standardize replacement, leakage, fill, and contamination checks
Machine Hopper-to-chamber seal-joint design and O-ring dimensions Matched repeat leakage with seal-joint structure Redesign item Use the existing spare for limited production and transfer redesign
Material O-ring chemical-resistance requirement Compared supplier specification and chemical-response results with the requirement Change requirement Reflect in material and dimensional requirements
Method Spare replacement and checks for leakage, fill quantity, and seal contamination Compared production results before and after replacement Temporary production condition Apply as limited-production standard work
Measurement Fill quantity, leakage, and seal-area contamination Checked each product under one work basis Limited-production evidence Inspect and record each item during production

The existing spare was replaced, and operator checks for leakage, fill quantity, and seal-area contamination were standardized. Supplier dimensions and chemical-reaction results were used to transfer a combined O-ring material/dimension and hopper-to-chamber joint change through the punch list and RFQ.

Crimping & Sizing

Crimping & Sizing mechanically seals the can and cap and establishes final cell height and geometry. One press did not consistently reach the target height, requiring two to three repetitions and causing height variation, crimp-shape defects, can/cap deformation, 3-jaw sticking, and cycle stops.

Repeated-press and geometry-variation disposition

4M Candidate Evidence Decision Action
Man Variation in repeated pressing, removal, and reinsertion Compared operator sequence with height and geometry results Recurrence-control factor Standardize repeated pressing, reinsertion, and full inspection
Machine HMI torque near its upper range, misaligned load centers, and 3-jaw sticking Correlated pressure sheets, torque, cycle stops, and deformation Primary structural cause Transfer drive, load-transfer, and 3-jaw redesign
Material Can and cap deformation Compared deformation by load condition and checked for material changes Material itself was not the primary cause Keep materials and prioritize load-transfer correction
Method Grease quantity, motion speed, two-to-three presses, and reinsertion Compared height, shape, and cycle results by condition Limited-production condition Apply lubrication, slower motion, and repeated pressing as standard work
Measurement Pressure-sensitive sheets, height, inner diameter, appearance, sealing, leakage, drop, impact, and vent Cross-checked product results with structural abnormalities Limited-production evidence Apply product-by-product disposition

Grease quantity and motion speed were adjusted, and repeated pressing, product removal/reinsertion, and full inspection were standardized. Drive-system, load-transfer-center, and 3-jaw changes were transferred as permanent improvement actions.

Marking

Marking applies lot, QR, and date information to the product and connects it with MES traceability. The original program fixed the format and did not allow a new QR pattern to be registered or an operator template to be changed.

New-pattern response disposition

4M Candidate Evidence Decision Action
Man Omission and input-error risk in manual lot/QR entry and MES recording Checked entry, marking, reading, and MES-record steps Recurrence-control factor Standardize the full sequence
Machine Software lacked new-pattern registration and user editing Checked attempted pattern/template changes against program functions Primary structural cause Transfer as a software improvement action
Material No material change was directly linked to marking results Risk concentrated in software function and manual entry Not selected as a primary cause Retain existing material and surface conditions
Method Manual lot/QR entry, manual marking, and manual MES recording Checked reading and records by manual step Limited-production condition Apply entry, marking, scanner, and MES recording as one standard
Measurement Visual inspection and scanner reading Checked appearance and readability immediately after marking Limited-production evidence Apply visual and scanner criteria

Operators entered the lot and QR data, ran marking, checked the result visually and with a scanner, and recorded it in MES under one standard procedure. New-pattern registration, input templates, and user-editing functions were transferred as software improvement actions.

Washing retained its existing conditions because no representative issue blocked the production transition.

Production validation and limited-production approval

  • Preliminary retest: Two 100-cell lots were run through the complete 4695 assembly process to revalidate equipment motion, welding/inspection results, and process-to-process connections.
  • Final continuous run: A final 50 cells were produced continuously from the first to the last operation under a defined combination of automatic and manual work.
  • Operating conditions: V3/V4 automatic inspection; V1/V2/V5 visual inspection and MES entry; filling leak/fill checks; repeated crimping; and manual marking were managed as standard work.

Based on the validation results and work standards, I submitted the technical recommendation for the limited-production range. Development, quality, manufacturing, and manufacturing-innovation managers approved the scope, completing the production-readiness handoff.

Standardization and handoff

  • Work standards, QCP, and P-FMEA
  • Manufacturing check sheets and quality measurement standards
  • Equipment punch lists, reference samples, and limit samples
  • MES key-control items and manual-entry procedures
  • Visual-inspection and operator-training materials
  • E/L Filling spare-parts list, modification request, and RFQ

I prepared the first drafts and aligned the field-use scope with engineering, maintenance, manufacturing, and quality. Production checks and manual work were separated from permanent improvement actions so the next owners could operate and continue the redesign under the same basis.

Final results

Three laser-welding processes, X-ray, AI-vision V3/V4, and Ready conditions were stabilized for the actual new product. AI-vision V1/V2/V5, E/L Filling, Crimping & Sizing, and Marking were brought into a defined production range through standardized manual work and inspection controls.

Two 100-cell lots and the final 50-cell continuous full-process run validated the limited-production setup, followed by management approval and handoff of the field standards.
