Equipment condition at handoff

The product and equipment SAT had been completed before the 4695 program entered production readiness in February 2025. Development and engineering should have overlapped during the transfer, but the previous development owners had left. The engineering team therefore had to adjust the equipment and repeat product trials.

No machine ran continuously from Start to completion. Operators moved each unit manually, and temporary recipes had accumulated around equipment that was not aligned to its original geometry. Product geometry, reflectivity, and turn count had changed, while X-ray and vision inspection still reflected the original SAT product.

The work started by restoring the equipment and measurement basis, not by repeatedly changing recipes. Each machine’s intended product result, geometry, alignment, inspection method, and process conditions were checked again with actual cells.

Sequence of work

  1. Interviewed operators and manufacturing staff about manual steps and repeated machine errors.
  2. Restated the intended product result and inspection basis for each machine.
  3. Restored jig contact, alignment, and machine geometry on the three laser-welding machines.
  4. Reset process and inspection conditions only after the equipment returned to its intended position.
  5. Compared X-ray and vision output with cell teardown and section results.
  6. Defined temporary work and inspection methods for filling, crimping, and marking machines that required redesign.
  7. Retested the full process with two 100-cell lots, then ran a final 50 cells continuously from the first to the last operation.
  8. Updated field documents and reviewed the production range with the responsible departments.

Three laser-welding machines

All three machines used temporary conditions around equipment geometry that was not properly aligned. Jig contact, collector position, and alignment were restored first; process conditions were then reset.

Collector Laser Welding

The joined layer range varied widely even during manual operation. Initial samples were inspected by separating layers, while the revised condition was checked through teardown and remaining electrode material. Because sample counts and measurement methods differed, the layer counts were not used as a numerical before-and-after result.

The public result is that equipment geometry and collector alignment were reset, the joining condition was revised to reduce variation while maintaining internal resistance, and the joint was checked by section, microscopy, and teardown.

Taping & Can Insert & Can Collector Welding

The effective joining area, penetration, tensile strength, and appearance between the positive collector and the positive can terminal were insufficient. After resetting machine geometry and process conditions, five samples were sectioned and etched for microscopic penetration measurement. Five additional samples were used for tensile strength and fracture-location checks.

Internal diameters and settings are omitted. The result is limited to the enlarged effective weld and the section, tensile, and fracture checks.

Beading & Collector Welding

The negative collector was offset from the can beading center, and the detection angle did not match the actual weld position. The detectable angle and weld position were corrected, then five initial samples and two lots were checked for position detection and automatic operation.

This is described as five initial samples plus two-lot confirmation, not as a 100-percent inspection-performance claim.

Internal laser-entry risk

Laser entry was managed as an equipment-specific internal-damage risk rather than one common acceptance item.

  • Collector Laser Welding used cell teardown to check J/R and separator damage.
  • Taping & Can Collector Welding did not have a suitable teardown sample for a separate laser-entry conclusion.
  • Beading & Collector Welding moved the weld position outward and checked internal damage where teardown samples were available.

X-ray and vision inspection

X-ray settings

The new product changed turn count, material, and internal geometry, but the original Count and ROI settings remained. An engineering team member performed the X-ray settings, cell teardown, sectioning, and microscopy. I set the trial direction, decided whether the product was actually abnormal, reviewed the retest, and coordinated the application decision.

X-ray output was compared with teardown and section results, then automatic inspection was applied for turn count, electrode overlap, and overhang.

The YOLO models trained for the original SAT product could not be applied to several new-product locations. There was no internal model-training owner, confidential product and laser geometry could not be transferred outside the company, and the vision supplier did not provide a training guide, model source, or incremental-training function.

The public description therefore does not claim that all five vision locations were recovered. Two locations used automatic inspection through the existing model and mechanical adjustment; the remaining three used operator visual inspection and manual MES entry.

A physical master jig was requested but rejected until the final product was fixed. A product made with the previous pattern was used as a reference sample, with position, illumination, focus, and limit-sample handling documented.

Automatic-ready conditions and measurement

Ready conditions

Operators initially checked or reset product sensing, axis home, and Ready conditions one by one. Operator, door, and laser safety remained active while maintenance and engineering revised the PLC sequence so the machine entered Ready after the required conditions were satisfied.

  • Maintenance checked and modified PLC sequence and interlock logic.
  • I defined Ready requirements from the equipment-acceptance basis and process purpose and supported the PLC changes.
  • Manufacturing operated the equipment and confirmed that trained operators could follow one preparation sequence.

Machines that still required redesign

The following machines were not fully stabilized during my employment. Temporary work methods and product inspection supported the production schedule; permanent mechanical or software changes remained outstanding.

E/L Filling

Electrolyte leakage, insufficient and variable fill quantity, and product contamination occurred at the hopper-to-chamber sealing connection. The spare O-ring size was checked with the equipment vendor and compared with domestic standard catalogues and specialist suppliers. A same-size component obtained through the supply source reacted during acidic and alkaline exposure tests and did not support the required chemical-resistance condition.

No formal material certification was performed, so the public description does not identify the material conclusively. Applying the required material would also have required changes to O-ring size and the hopper-to-chamber joint. The modification request and punch item were rejected. Production continued through spare replacement and operator checks for low fill and seal-area contamination.

Crimping & Sizing

HMI torque remained near its maximum range and one press did not reach target height, requiring two to three repetitions. The result was height variation, crimp-shape defects, can/cap deformation, 3-jaw sticking, scratches, cycle stops, and a process bottleneck.

Pressure-sensitive sheets were checked twice by me, maintenance, and an engineering team member and showed that the upper and lower load centers did not coincide. The drive and load-transfer structure could not be changed during employment. Grease quantity was increased and motion speed reduced; after repeated pressing, total height, inner diameter, appearance, airtightness, leakage, drop, impact, and vent performance were inspected.

Marking

The program fixed the lot, QR, and date formats, and a new QR pattern could not be registered through MES. Operators manually entered lot and QR data, ran marking, checked it visually and with a scanner, and entered the result into MES.

There was no confirmed dataset showing zero omissions, duplicates, or misreads in the final 50 cells, so no zero-defect result is claimed. New-pattern registration and user-editing functions remained software changes for a later stage.

Washing had no major issue that prevented the production transition and is not presented as a separate improvement item.
