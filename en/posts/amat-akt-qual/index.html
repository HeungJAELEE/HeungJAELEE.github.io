Manufacturing case study

Field application

Technical Qualification of CVD and PECVD Components

Aligning same-sample measurement results before documenting approved process and inspection criteria

Project period
Qualification work during YMC employment
Context
Completed in YMC's Technical R&D Center
Scope
Process and inspection specification rebuild, measurement correlation, sample validation, and technical audit support

Field problem and result summary

Field problem
Stable internal results were insufficient when customer measurement methods and acceptance criteria differed.
What I owned
I rebuilt surface-treatment and inspection criteria, including Hi-Pot, acid resistance, and corrosion resistance, and supported same-sample correlation and technical audits.
Verified result
Same-sample measurement correlation was established, and the technical qualification conditions were added to QCPs, run sheets, and inspection standards.

Why the problem mattered

A semiconductor or display-equipment component can pass an internal inspection and still fail customer qualification when instruments, locations, or acceptance methods differ. The same samples showed different thickness and electrical-inspection results across the two measurement environments. Surface treatment, insulation, acid resistance, and corrosion resistance therefore required process and measurement methods to be aligned together so that a qualification result could be repeated in production.

Initial state and measurement frame

I did not begin by changing the process recipe. Without understanding why internal and customer measurements differ, a process change can mix real process variation with measurement variation.

The work was separated into three layers:

  1. Process: pretreatment, surface treatment, cleaning, and post-treatment
  2. Product: film thickness, roughness, appearance, insulation, and environmental resistance
  3. Measurement: instrument, sample position, repeats, decision method, and bias

Separating qualification mismatches with 4M

Changing the production recipe as soon as customer measurements differed would mix process and measurement causes. I used 4M to classify the possible mismatch before setting the same-sample trial sequence.

4MIssue reviewedCheck direction
ManSample position, reading, and interpretation of customer requirements could vary by inspectorAligned measurement location, repeat count, and acceptance method
MachineInternal and customer instruments showed different Ramp Time and Arc Count results and repeatabilityRepeated measurements on the same samples to review instrument bias and repeatability
MaterialDifferences in sample material, surface, film, or pretreatment could look like instrument differencesCompared the same samples in the same pretreatment state
MethodEnglish-spec interpretation, pretreatment, inspection sequence, and acceptance rules were not fully linked to the process conditionConnected requirements, process conditions, and inspection items in one table

This prevented a machine-related measurement difference from being treated as a recipe problem. Process changes followed only after measurement-method and sample differences had been addressed.

Running the qualification through DMAIC

  1. Define — Set the gap between global equipment-maker requirements and internally explained process and inspection results as the qualification problem.
  2. Measure — Repeated thickness, roughness, dielectric strength, Arc Count, Ramp Time, and related checks on the same samples and recorded location, repeats, and bias.
  3. Analyze — Used 4M to separate actual process variation, sample differences, and instrument or inspection-method effects.
  4. Improve — Aligned Bead Blast and oxalic-anodizing conditions and inspection methods with the customer specification and retested corrective items.
  5. Control — Added qualified conditions to recipes, run sheets, process standards, and inspection criteria for production use.

Actions I performed

Aligning process and inspection items

I reviewed the customer’s English specification and connected Bead Blast and oxalic-anodizing conditions to the required checks. Thickness, roughness, color difference, dielectric strength, Arc Count, Ramp Time, particles, and dimensions were organized by product need; unresolved items remained sample-validation tasks.

Comparing the same samples

Different samples would mix product and instrument effects. The same samples were repeatedly measured internally and by the customer to review bias and repeatability, while measurement locations and pretreatment were aligned. Process capability and measurement-system reliability remained separate questions.

Tracking technical-audit actions

Customer technical-audit findings were separated into process, inspection, and document actions. Revised conditions were retested on samples before being added to standard documents.

Verification sequence

  1. Break down customer requirements.
  2. Connect each requirement to process conditions and inspection items.
  3. Repeat measurements on the same samples.
  4. Separate measurement bias from process differences.
  5. Validate the selected conditions with samples.
  6. Close technical-audit actions and complete qualification.
  7. Update recipes, run sheets, and inspection standards.

Changes were controlled so multiple conditions and measurement methods were not changed at once. The reason, sample, measurement location, and acceptance method were recorded for comparison.

Results

Technical result

Surface-treatment, Hi-Pot, acid-resistance, and corrosion-resistance criteria for CVD/PECVD process components were rebuilt around global equipment-maker requirements and taken through qualification. Confirmed conditions were documented in recipes, run sheets, process standards, and inspection criteria.

What I carried into later manufacturing work

  • When customer measurements differ, compare the same sample and measurement method before changing the process.
  • Convert qualification conditions into shop-floor recipes and inspection standards.
  • Evaluate technical approval, production application, and business profitability separately.
