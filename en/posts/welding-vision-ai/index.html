    The first minute

    This case is a training-team Vision AI PoC that classifies weld images into normal and defect candidates. It was not a system that replaced inspectors or made shipment decisions. The team evaluated whether a model could narrow the images that an inspector should review first.

    Why weld-defect detection matters

    A missed micro-crack or pinhole can allow a defective weld to continue downstream. Flagging a normal part as a defect candidate creates reinspection work. Both have a cost, but this project gave priority to reducing the error that passes a defect as normal.

    When normal images greatly outnumber defect images, a model can report high accuracy by predicting normal most of the time. The evaluation therefore did not stop at “How many images were correct?” It considered recall—the share of actual defects found—together with precision, which helps describe the reinspection load.

    My battery-cell assembly experience with ultrasonic and laser welding informed that priority. The field experience and the education-project metrics remain separate, however. This case evaluates inspection-assistance potential on bounded material; it does not claim completed autonomous inspection.

    How data, models, and evaluation connect

    1. Separate the purpose of each data source

    • Approximately 60,000 AI-Hub public welding images supplied broad learning coverage.
    • 198 field-practice images provided a separate, limited check under field capture conditions.

    The two sources were not presented as one undifferentiated set of 60,198 training images. Cameras, illumination, surface reflections, and product conditions differed. Strong results on public data were not treated as proof of the same behavior in the field.

    2. Check labels and splits

    The workflow aligned the meaning of public labels with the field defect definition and kept the same image from appearing in both learning and evaluation. If that boundary fails, a high score may only show that the model saw the same image again.

    3. Compare the team’s model stages on one basis

    The team experiment record progressed from a DenseNet-stage baseline through a two-model combination to a ResNet+DeiT ensemble. The stages were compared under the same F1, recall, and precision frame instead of changing the success rule for each model. I do not present all of that model development as solo work.

    4. Turn probabilities into an inspection rule

    The model outputs a defect probability for each image. A threshold decides when that probability becomes a defect candidate. Lowering the threshold can catch more defects, but it can also send more normal images to reinspection.

    TermPlain meaningWhy it mattered here
    RecallThe share of actual defects the model foundReduce missed defects first
    PrecisionThe share of defect candidates that were actual defectsUnderstand the reinspection load
    F1A balance between recall and precisionAvoid choosing from one favorable metric
    False negativeA defect predicted as normalThe error reduced first
    False positiveA normal image predicted as a defect candidateThe error connected to reinspection cost

    5. Look back at the images behind the metrics

    The second test reviewed the region where normal and defect predictions mixed. The evaluation first found thresholds that retained 100% recall, compared precision and F1 within that region, and then reviewed FP and FN images to identify the next questions for data, labels, and capture conditions.

    Verified result and potential benefits

    At the selected threshold on the recorded evaluation set, the report lists F1 93.10%, Recall 100%, and Precision 87.07%. Recall 100% means that no defect in that evaluation set was missed. It does not guarantee every future defect or every equipment condition. Precision 87.07% shows that some defect candidates may be normal and require reinspection.

    The benefit demonstrated by the PoC was not one metric in isolation. It was a way to turn quality risk into an inspection policy:

    • a review priority that puts higher-cost candidates in front of an inspector first;
    • one evaluation frame for comparing model candidates;
    • a shared language that links missed defects to quality risk and false alarms to reinspection; and
    • a sequence for returning recurring error images to data and capture improvement.

    A reduction in escaped defects or inspection time was not measured. Such effects require a controlled comparison before and after production introduction.

    Current limitations and next validation

    The current stage is a bounded PoC. The 198 field images do not cover every equipment, material, lighting, and product-shape condition. Real-time camera input, inference at line speed, dropped frames, long-term performance change, and the operator reinspection procedure were not validated.

    Field use would require the following checks:

    1. Build an independent evaluation set split by product or lot.
    2. Reconfirm class-level sample counts and label rules with quality specialists.
    3. Standardize camera position, illumination, focus, and capture timing.
    4. Test inference latency and dropped frames at line speed.
    5. Trace model version, threshold, image, equipment condition, and reinspection result together.
    6. Provide change approval and a path back to an earlier model and threshold.

    Design choices and work flow

    The project is defined as a missed-defect risk decision, not an accuracy contest, and connects data, model, threshold, and reinspection tasks under one evaluation policy.

    Problem

    Micro-defect features overlap normal surfaces and the data is imbalanced, so accuracy alone does not represent the cost of passing a defective weld.

    Goal

    Separate public and field data, compare model stages on the same metrics, and design a recall-first threshold with a reinspection policy.

    Success

    F1, recall, and precision are reported together with threshold rationale, false-positive handling, and explicit production limits.

    Scope and boundaries

    In scope

    • Purpose separation for about 60,000 AI-Hub images and 198 field images
    • DenseNet baseline, two-model combination, and ResNet+DeiT comparison
    • Second test and threshold tuning around missed-defect risk

    Out of scope or unverified

    • Claiming a dedicated public source repository
    • Generalization to every weld process and camera condition
    • Shipment decisions with no human reinspection path

    Implementation and verification

    What had to work

    • Separate data source and training, validation, and field-review purpose.
    • Compare model stages with the same F1, recall, precision, and split policy.
    • Record false negatives and false positives across thresholds and retain reinspection.

    Quality and operating rules

    • Prioritize missed-defect cost over the cost of reinspecting a normal part.
    • Separate evaluation-set results from production claims and check leakage.
    • Treat lighting, material, and camera drift as separate validation work.

    Why it was designed this way

    1. Use recall as the primary operating metric

      Passing a defective weld carries more quality risk than reinspecting a normal part.

    2. Keep 198 field images as a distinct validation view

      Small field data should not be mixed into a large public set to imply broad generalization.

    3. Compare threshold policy with model choice

      The same model produces different miss and reinspection costs at different thresholds.
