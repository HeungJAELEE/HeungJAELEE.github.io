Project objective

The project started with public ship and offshore-plant welding images and then evaluated the models on weld specimens produced during the Facility Maintenance Engineer practical welding exercise. The objective was not merely to select the model with the highest score on public data. It was to analyze errors under different capture conditions, form an effective AI modeling approach, and connect the result to a smartphone app demonstration.

The team designed a training inspection-assistance system that receives a weld image and displays good/defect status and defect grade. Quality-standard research, data preparation, model training and evaluation, threshold selection, and a PWA demonstration were connected in one flow.

Data sources and separated evaluation sets

The source-data table in the report lists about 60,000 undercut and overlap images. A later training table records 56,256 usable ship-component images after preparation. Directly captured practical weld specimens were used in experiment-specific sets of about 155 to 198 images, or roughly 200 images in total scale.

Because the number and capture timing differed across experiments, metrics from different report tables were not merged into one final score. Only before-and-after changes within the same evaluation stage were compared.

Domain gap between public data and practical specimens

Public images had relatively consistent lighting and viewing angles and cleaner weld surfaces. The practical specimens included soot, spatter, oxidation, reflections, background clutter, and angle variation. The initial model had learned the cleaner appearance of the public data and either treated field noise as a defect or missed real defects.

The public learning material and the directly captured specimens differed in surface condition, lighting, angle, and background noise.

In the initial ResNet evaluation on captured specimens, accuracy was 38.86%, F1 was 21.33%, recall was 15.84%, and FPR was 35.87%. Before changing the architecture, the gap was separated as a data and capture-condition problem.

How the images were evaluated

Model scores were reviewed together with source images and error types.

  1. Predictions were compared with the original image and reference label.
  2. False-negative images, in which real defects were passed as normal, were reviewed first.
  3. False positives were classified by soot, spatter, reflection, viewing angle, and ROI displacement.
  4. The same evaluation set was rerun at different thresholds to compare changes in FN and FP.
  5. Low-confidence and gray-zone cases were routed to recapture or operator review rather than automatic acceptance.

Augmentation and additional learning for captured conditions

Crop, flip, rotation, and perspective transforms represented framing changes. Brightness, contrast, gamma, shadow, and lens-flare augmentation represented lighting variation. Blur, Gaussian noise, JPEG artifacts, and partial occlusion represented capture-quality degradation. MixUp, label smoothing, SWA, multi-seed averaging, and temperature scaling were also reviewed during learning and calibration.

The report capture shows framing, lighting, and noise augmentation together with MixUp, label smoothing, SWA, multi-seed averaging, and temperature scaling.

The largest change was recall, which rose from 15.84% to 85.15%, a gain of 69.31 percentage points. This showed that data representing the actual capture conditions had to be addressed before adding newer architectures.

Candidate comparison and final model combination

ResNet, Swin, DeiT, ConvNeXt, EfficientNet, and RegNet families were compared using the same evaluation items. CNN models were relatively stable for local features and conservative good-part decisions, while Transformer models tended to follow the global weld-bead pattern and detect defects more sensitively.

The numerical evaluation and app demo used a ResNet-50 + DeiT-Small two-model ensemble with uniform probability averaging. The report records F1 of 96.08% for a five-model ensemble and 96.04% for the two-model version, a difference of 0.04 percentage points, while inference cost was summarized as approximately one third.

Turning the threshold into an inspection condition

Threshold 0.49 produced the highest F1, but four false negatives remained in the limited evaluation set. Lowering the threshold to 0.40 reduced FN to zero and raised recall to 100%, while FP increased from four to fifteen.

The same specimen changes grade as the threshold changes. Lower thresholds increase defect-candidate sensitivity and also increase the operator-review load.

The demonstration used threshold 0.40 to prioritize missed-defect reduction. Within this limited set, FN fell from four to zero, a 100% reduction, and recall moved from 96.0% to 100.0%, a gain of 4.0 percentage points. FP increased from four to fifteen, and FPR rose from 4.3% to 16.3%, a gain of 12.0 percentage points.

The final demonstration table records accuracy of 92.23%, precision of 87.07%, recall of 100%, and F1 of 93.10%. These are results under the selected threshold on the report’s limited evaluation set, not evidence of zero escapes across a production line.

App-form inspection demonstration

The report architecture sends the smartphone image to /predict, averages ResNet-50 and DeiT-Small outputs, and returns the result as JSON.

A smartphone image was sent as HTTPS multipart/form-data to the FastAPI /predict endpoint. The server averaged ResNet-50 and DeiT-Small predictions and applied temperature scaling and the selected threshold. The PWA displayed grade, defect_type, and confidence from the JSON response.

The app demonstrated A, C, and D grades together with confidence. The result was used as inspection-assistance information that an operator could review with the source image.

The report demonstration displays grade, defect type, and confidence beside the source image.

Modeling and evaluation decisions I owned

  • Planned the overall project objective and model-evaluation flow.
  • Separated the domain gap between public data and directly captured practical specimens.
  • Compared candidate models using recall, FPR, F1, and error images under the same criteria.
  • Developed coordinate-scaling logic so low-resolution inputs remained aligned with the original image and ROI.
  • Compared FN and FP across thresholds and selected the demonstration decision condition.
  • Framed the team result as an app input, inference, result display, and operator-review flow rather than a model score alone.

Team result and next validation

The five-person team progressed from AI-Hub learning material to directly captured practical weld specimens, domain-gap analysis, augmentation, candidate comparison, a ResNet+DeiT ensemble, threshold adjustment, and a FastAPI/PWA demonstration.

The next stage requires an independent set separated by product, lot, and capture time; fixed camera, lighting, and distance; inference-time checks; and recapture rules. A production shadow test can then measure missed defects, false alarms, and operator review load without automatically scrapping product.
