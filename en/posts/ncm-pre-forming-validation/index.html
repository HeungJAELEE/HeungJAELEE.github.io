Why Pre-Forming was required

During early NCM pilot production, tearing, active-material shedding, and shorts repeatedly appeared around the positive- and negative-electrode uncoated edges after ultrasonic pre-welding. Reducing the welding condition lowered damage but also weakened the required joint. Manual pre-forming showed the correct direction, but position and R geometry changed by operator.

The formal process name is Pre-Forming. The process shapes the two uncoated edges before pre-welding according to their material behavior and replaces operator feel with jig, geometry, position, and inspection criteria.

My direct work covered damage reproduction, positive/negative cause separation, alternative disposition, pilot-jig concept and drawing, R geometry and forming-position definition, sample build, teardown and functional validation, process documents, training, and the production-equipment RFQ.

Why the weaker-welding alternative was rejected

The process had to protect the uncoated edges while retaining joint quality. I therefore kept the pre-welding joint requirement and designed a stable forming step before welding rather than lowering the weld condition.

Separating positive- and negative-electrode damage mechanisms

The positive and negative failure modes did not share one mechanism. Teardown location and geometry were compared before defining separate process conditions.

Completion required no short, no tearing, shedding, or excessive compression after teardown, while pre-weld appearance and tensile results retained the required level.

Pilot-jig design and conversion of operator variation

One manually formed batch first confirmed the damage-reduction direction. Even trained operators, however, generally showed about 1.5 mm of position variation, and differences in direction and pressure changed R geometry and damage location.

Material-specific mechanismPositive foil tear and negative heavy-edge sheddingSeparate R geometry and forming position
Operator position spreadAbout 1.5 mm variation even among trained operatorsDatum, stopper, and correct-insertion position
Pressure and direction spreadR geometry and damage location changed with pressing directionInsertion direction, sequence, and hold criterion

Inspection blind spotAppearance alone did not reveal internal damageLink Short, Hi-Pot, IR, tensile, and teardown

When a new operator inserted the J/R incorrectly and reproduced the same damage, jig setting and correct insertion were retrained and checked again under the same inspection basis. Operator skill was no longer the control; the jig and sequence absorbed normal placement variation.

Validation across about 400 samples

Action and retest

Exploratory condition narrowing and selected-condition confirmation were kept as separate stages.

StageScopePurpose
Initial explorationabout 200 initial disposable samplesNarrow R geometry, forming position, and insertion candidates
Selected-condition confirmationfour lots × 50 cells = 200 cellsApply one basis across operator and lot changes
Total validation scopeabout 400 samplesCombined exploration and selected-condition confirmation

The selected pilot-jig, R-geometry, and insertion conditions were applied to four lots × 50 cells and checked through one disposition flow:

  • Short, Hi-Pot, and IR
  • Pre-weld appearance, tensile result, and fracture mode
  • Teardown condition of the uncoated edge and adjacent coating
  • Formation, capacity, cycling, and life
  • Correct insertion and damage recurrence across operator and lot changes

Result and standardization

The selected criteria were later used on pilot samples, operator changes, other products and sizes, and customer-submission samples under the same decision principle. Geometry, position, correct insertion, and inspection criteria confirmed with the pilot jig were transferred to process standards and follow-on production-equipment requirements.
