    TECHNICAL CASE STUDY

    New Process Development for Short-Circuit Prevention

    A pilot-process design case that retained the pre-welding joint requirement, separated positive Al-foil tearing from negative heavy-edge shedding and shorts, and validated pilot-jig criteria across about 400 samples and four lots

    Aug 5, 2026 · 5 minutes · Last reviewed Aug 10, 2026

    • Pre-Forming
    • Pilot Jig
    • Failure Mechanism

    Project background and problem definition

    During early NCM pilot production, ultrasonic pre-welding repeatedly produced positive-electrode uncoated-edge tearing, active-material shedding around the negative heavy edge, and shorts. Reducing the welding condition lowered damage but also weakened the required joint. Manual pre-forming showed a workable direction, but position, pressure, and R geometry changed by operator.

    Process decision: I rejected a condition compromise that traded joint strength for lower damage. The selected direction retained the pre-welding requirement, separated the damage mechanism by electrode, and controlled forming position and geometry through a pilot jig.

    My direct scope covered damage reproduction and teardown analysis, positive/negative cause separation, pilot-jig concept and drawing, R geometry and forming-position definition, sample builds and inspection, process documents, training, and the production-equipment RFQ.

    • Environment: Process-environment change · No repeated relationship with damage location or form · Removed as primary cause · Retain existing environmental controls

    Core process engineering

    1. Separate the electrode-specific damage mechanisms

    I did not treat positive- and negative-electrode failures as one forming problem. Teardown location and damage geometry were used to define separate process criteria.

    AreaObserved failure modeProcess-design decision
    Positive electrodeAl-foil uncoated-edge tear and fracturePositive-specific R geometry and a gentler forming position
    Negative electrodeActive-material shedding and excessive compression around the heavy edgeNegative-specific R geometry and compression-limiting position
    Common acceptanceShort and weld-quality riskOne completion basis linking Short, Hi-Pot, IR, weld appearance/tensile, and teardown

    The weaker-welding alternative was rejected because tensile performance fell together with damage. Forming through the ultrasonic-welder motion was also rejected due to higher takt and added J/R damage risk. Pre-forming was therefore separated as its own operation.

    2. Design the pilot jig and control operator variation

    One manually formed batch confirmed the damage-reduction direction. The next step converted position, pressure, and insertion variation into mechanical and work criteria.

    Field variationPilot-jig and work responseVerification
    Operator forming-position variationDatum, stopper, and correct-insertion positionPosition, appearance, short, and teardown
    Direction and pressure differencesElectrode-specific R geometry, insertion direction, and work sequenceCoated-area compression and foil tearing
    Incorrect J/R insertionCorrect-insertion and hold criteriaRetraining and retest for a new operator
    Appearance blind spotLinked electrical, weld, teardown, and cell evaluationHi-Pot, IR, tensile, formation, and life

    I prepared the pilot-jig concept and drawing and defined the positive/negative R geometry, forming position, and correct-insertion criteria. When incorrect insertion by a new operator reproduced the same damage, jig setting and insertion sequence were retrained and retested on the same basis.

    3. Validate pilot processability across about 400 samples

    Action and retest

    Exploratory trials for narrowing geometry were separated from lot-level confirmation of the selected condition.

    StageScopePurpose
    Initial explorationAbout 200 initial disposable samplesNarrow R geometry, forming position, and insertion candidates
    Selected-condition confirmationfour lots × 50 cells = 200 cellsConfirm the same basis across operator and lot changes
    Total validation scopeAbout 400 samplesExploration plus selected-condition confirmation

    The selected pilot-jig, R-geometry, and insertion conditions were checked through one validation flow.

    Appearance and correct insertion
→ Short, Hi-Pot, and IR
→ pre-weld appearance, tensile, and fracture mode
→ uncoated-edge and coated-area teardown
→ formation, capacity, cycling, and life

    Result and standardization

    The selected condition was confirmed on the same inspection basis across operator and lot changes. The same decision logic was then used for subsequent pilot samples, other products and sizes, and customer-submission samples.

    Final result and production-equipment handoff

    AreaResult
    Trade-off resolutionPre-welding requirement retained · Pilot condition meeting uncoated-edge damage and short criteria
    Operator-variation controlManual position, pressure, and insertion variation converted into jig, datum, stopper, and work-sequence criteria
    ValidationAbout 200 exploratory samples plus four lots×50 selected-condition cells, about 400 samples in total
    StandardizationWork standard, QCP, P-FMEA, manufacturing checklist, in-process criteria, and training material
    Scale-upPilot-jig geometry, position, insertion, and inspection requirements transferred into the production-equipment RFQ and design/fabrication requirements

    The pilot process condition was fixed in work and inspection standards, and the RFQ required the follow-on equipment to reproduce the same positioning and inspection flow.
