2170 Assembly-Line Good-Yield Stabilization

A production improvement that separated measurement error from process defects before controlled retests and standardization

Project period
Core stabilization May–Dec 2024 · follow-up Jan–Feb 2025
Context
Completed while working in Kumyang's Battery Technology Team
Scope
Aligned production, quality, equipment, and development teams around yield, defect, downtime, and retest evidence

Field problem and result summary

Field problem
Displayed yield and shop-floor loss did not agree, so teams prioritized different causes.
What I owned
As assembly-process lead, I aligned denominators and defect names, then coordinated MSA, controlled retests, and cross-team actions.
Verified result
Monthly average good yield reached 98.7% in December 2024 from an initial level of about 40%, followed by SOP, QCP, and P-FMEA updates.
Good yield About 40% → 98.7%

Problem and context

Losses were occurring across filling, resistance welding, vision, and visual inspection at the same time. In a batch of about 2,000 cells, filling-related loss was roughly 150–220 cells, while post-formation IR defects were about 4%. Equipment-screen judgements did not always match what the shop floor saw, so the first question was whether to change the process or recheck the measurement method.

Counting measurement error as a product defect would lead to the wrong process changes; treating a real defect as an inspection problem would make it recur. I therefore started by aligning production quantity, inspected quantity, defect categories, downtime, and measurement rules rather than tuning one recipe from a headline yield number.

What I owned

I standardized the yield calculation as total good cells ÷ total input cells and combined production, inspection, defect, and downtime records under the same definitions. Because the initial EOL judgement was not reliable enough, more than ten batches were fully inspected and Gage R&R and maintenance MSA were repeated. The master jig’s zero, scale, location, and measurement sequence were also checked before measurement misclassification was separated from actual defects.

The review order was measurement and sensors → vision and inspection → jigs and consumables → recipe. Filling work first checked jig alignment, O-ring contact and sealing, the clamping structure, and spring condition. Only after the structure was fixed did the trials compare valve-close, pressure, vacuum, and impregnation sequences.

Resistance-welding work separated no-weld, weak-weld, and appearance issues. Alignment and head condition were checked before constant-current settings, pressure, electrode state, and part-shape variation were compared. After incomplete welds were identified as one cause of formation IR defects, welding and electrode-management standards were adjusted. Equipment, jig, lot, defect, and O-ring replacement history was linked in Excel/VBA to find recurring conditions.

Separating the causes with 4M

Because losses appeared in several processes at once, I did not begin by changing equipment recipes. A 4M review showed that the same defect label could contain different causes.

MethodValve, pressure, vacuum, and impregnation sequence; welding settings; inspection method; and replacement cycle varied with experienceChanged one condition at a time and retested with the same samples and acceptance rule

The 4M table was a work map, not a list of assumed root causes. For filling loss, machine factors such as jig alignment and O-ring sealing were fixed before method factors such as the vacuum and pressure sequence were compared. Electrode material and welding settings were tested separately in the same way.

Managing the work with DMAIC

  1. Define — Set the initial good yield of about 40%, filling loss, and formation IR defects as priorities, then aligned the yield formula and defect names.
  2. Measure — Combined production, inspection, defect, and downtime records and used MSA/Gage R&R to separate misclassification from actual defects.
  3. Analyze — Broke the losses down with 4M to distinguish the effects of jigs, O-rings, electrodes, recipes, and inspection conditions.
  4. Improve — Fixed structural factors first, then changed filling sequences and welding settings one at a time and applied only repeatable results.
  5. Control — Moved confirmed conditions into SOPs, QCP, P-FMEA, inspection rules, and training, while retaining lot, replacement, and defect history.

Result and field application

The core stabilization ran from May through December 2024, with follow-up checks through February 2025. Monthly average good yield reached 98.7% in December 2024 from an initial level of about 40%, calculated as total good cells ÷ total input cells.

  • Formation IR defects fell from about 4% to roughly 0.3%.
  • Filling work fixed sealing, alignment, and clamping before material changes and Taguchi trials narrowed the recipe range.
  • Exact temperature/humidity causality for rust and other insufficiently documented items were excluded from headline results.
  • Confirmed settings were applied to SOPs, QCP, P-FMEA, inspection and self-check criteria, and training material.
  • MES fields and Cell Tracking requirements were documented for the responsible system team.

The 98.7% figure is a cross-functional team result. My part was to align definitions, separate measurement error from actual defects, and coordinate controlled trials and actions. I maintained existing assembly documents and reviewed new-product documents prepared by the responsible engineers.
