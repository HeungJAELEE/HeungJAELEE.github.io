Stabilizing 2170 Mass Production by Fixing Measurement First

A production improvement that separated measurement misclassification from process defects before controlled retests and standardization

Project period
Core stabilization May–Dec 2024 · follow-up Jan–Feb 2025
Context
Completed while working in Kumyang's Battery Technology Team
Scope
Aligned production, quality, equipment, and development teams around yield, defect, downtime, and retest evidence

Field problem and result summary

Field problem
Displayed yield, actual good yield, and shop-floor defects did not agree, so measurement and decision reliability had to be checked before process settings.
What I owned
I led assembly-process trials and cross-team coordination, narrowing causes from measurement and sensors through inspection, jigs, consumables, and finally recipes.
Verified result
The team recorded 98.7% monthly average good yield in December 2024, and I directly revised the MSA criteria, SOP, QCP, P-FMEA, and inspection standards.
Good yield About 40% → 98.7%

I fixed the decision system before changing the yield

Displayed yield, actual good yield, and the defects observed on the shop floor did not agree. The initial level of about 40% was calculated from cells judged fully good at the time divided by total input; I later rechecked those decisions through retrospective inspection. Changing process settings first would have mixed inspection misclassification with actual defects, so production, inspection, rework, scrap, and defect definitions were aligned first.

I fully inspected more than ten batches and reviewed the measurement system through Gage R&R and maintenance MSA criteria. The sequence was measurement and sensors → inspection and vision → jigs, alignment, and consumables → recipe. Process settings were changed one at a time only after measurement and equipment conditions were stable.

Good cells were being rejected by inspection settings

After Can Insert, Vision inspection repeatedly rejected cells at a particular rotation angle. Electrode-press thickness and product geometry were considered, but thickness remained within the designed process margin while false rejections clustered at one orientation. The priority therefore shifted to alignment by rotation angle and an overly narrow recognition margin.

SignalGood cells rejected at one rotation angle SeparationPress thickness remained within design margin CheckAlignment position shifted with rotation ActionRebuilt inspection area, good range, and settings RetestCompared decisions by orientation

I realigned the rotation direction and widened the inspection margin to account for electrode-thickness variation. The good-decision range, inspection area, NG screening conditions, and inner/outer-tab settings were revised together. The product inspection specification itself did not change. What changed was how the inspection system interpreted that specification, and the confirmed settings became controlled parameters.

I separated alignment and inspection criteria before welding settings

Incomplete negative-tab resistance welds and Formation IR defects appeared together. Changing only the process recipe would have mixed equipment alignment, head condition, electrode variation, and inspection criteria. I therefore checked alignment, head condition, electrode state, and whether the existing inspection method could distinguish actual weld quality before moving to process trials.

SignalIncomplete welds and Formation IR defects Mechanical checkAlignment, head, and electrode state Decision checkInspection criteria versus actual weld Controlled trialChanged one welding condition at a time RetestPQC, nugget, tensile strength, and Formation IR

After stabilizing the mechanical state and decision criteria, I ran welding-condition trials. Results were checked again through in-process PQC, nugget evaluation, tensile strength, and Formation IR. The output was not one recipe value but a repeatable control sequence covering alignment, electrodes, inspection, and process settings.

I did not treat leakage and filling variation as one cause

Leakage and underweight cells appeared together, but underweight cells also occurred without leakage. Treating both as one defect would have hidden the difference between structural and process-setting causes. In periods when filling-related loss reached roughly 150–220 cells in batches of about 2,000 cells, I began with the pattern by jig position.

SignalLeakage and underweight cells PatternVariation by jig position StructureSealing, alignment, and clamping structure ComponentsO-ring, spring, hopper, and pump SettingsPosition, pressure, vacuum, and impregnation trials

I first reviewed mechanical sealing-structure factors—sealing, alignment, and clamping structure together with O-ring contact and spring condition. Hopper and pump were then checked separately, while remaining variation was split by material change, position, pressure, vacuum, and impregnation settings. Leakage, fill quantity, and position variation were retested separately so that structural changes were not confused with recipe changes.

Rust and appearance marks were controlled through acceptance gates

Rust and downstream appearance marks were not assigned to one cause. Over-weld marks, tubing discoloration, and scratches were reviewed for rust formation and functional impact. Only conditions that passed the limit-sample test were accepted.

ObserveSeparate discoloration, weld marks, and scratches
Check functionReview rust formation and functional impact
Limit sampleAgree the acceptable appearance range
DecideRelease only conditions that passed the gate

Appearance marks without rust or functional impact were reviewed as acceptable. Scratches with a risk of internal ingress or customer complaints were excluded. Tests still in progress at the time, including the water-replacement-cycle study related to pH, were not presented as completed results.

In CXI X-Ray inspection, a cone-shaped rise of both electrodes during winding control repeatedly produced no-measurement decisions. After quality review determined that the actual cells were good, the count condition was adjusted. Cell disassembly and cross-section analysis then confirmed that actual winding defects or active-material-position abnormalities were not being passed as good. The change was implemented after approval by the development and engineering teams and the responsible director.

Retest results were fixed in five control documents

Confirmed results were not left as personal know-how. I directly revised all five control documents below so that the line could reuse the same checks.

The line stabilized from about 40% to a 98.7% monthly average good yield in December 2024 as a cross-functional team result. The formula was total good cells ÷ total input cells. My direct scope covered assembly-process trials, cross-team coordination, action schedules, and revision of existing-product control documents.

An item excluded from the representative result

For CPF Pre-Charge, I suspected future non-operation or mismeasurement after a PCB had been exposed to rain, but evidence did not close the relationship among board replacement, equipment behavior, and product condition. It is therefore excluded from the completed cases.

Conflicting temperature, humidity, and corrosion causality is not used as a cause or result in this case.
