2170 Assembly-Line Good-Yield Stabilization

Problem and context

Good-product yield on the 2170 assembly line was initially about 40%. Before increasing output, we had to distinguish whether losses came from the product, equipment, or inspection. Treating a gauge error as a process defect can lead to the wrong process change; treating a real defect as an inspection issue allows it to recur.

I therefore started by aligning production quantity, inspected quantity, defect categories, downtime, and measurement rules rather than tuning one recipe from a headline yield number.

What I owned

  • Managed the production plan, WIP, defect, and downtime view as the assembly-process lead.
  • Aligned the denominator and defect names used by production, quality, equipment, and development teams.
  • Reviewed MSA and Gage R&R with quality engineers to separate misclassification from actual process defects.
  • Tracked owners and retest dates for jig tolerances, recipes, wear parts, vision, and inspection conditions.
  • Applied confirmed conditions to SOPs, QCP, P-FMEA, inspection criteria, and operator training.

What I checked and changed

Check Why Action
Yield denominator and defect names Prevent departments from comparing different numbers Put production, inspection, retest, and scrap rules in one view
Measurement system Avoid counting misclassification as a process defect Reviewed MSA/Gage R&R and separated inspection and process causes
Jig and alignment Check tolerance and wear effects on position Refined jig tolerance and replacement/inspection rules
Recipes and wear parts Keep setting changes separate from physical wear Changed one variable at a time and retested under the same criteria
Vision and inspection Match detection conditions to the actual CTQ Rechecked decision conditions against the quality rule

The trial object, inspection method, and acceptance rule were held constant. A change reached the line only after a repeat check supported it.

Result and limits

  • The core stabilization ran from May through December 2024; monthly average good yield reached 98.7% in December from an initial level of about 40%.
  • The confirmed settings and checks were recorded so different operators and shifts could follow the same baseline.
  • I did not modify MES directly; I documented management-field and Cell Tracking requirements for the responsible system team.

The calculation is total good cells ÷ total input cells, and 98.7% is a team result. It is not a third-party recalculation from public raw data or a universal result for every 2170 line. Company drawings, recipes, equipment addresses, customer specifications, and raw quality data remain private.

In an interview I can explain the denominator, the MSA separation, the order of condition changes, and how the results entered standard work. Company records and raw production data will not be made public.
