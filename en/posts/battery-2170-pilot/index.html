The three-minute case

Why it mattered

Good-product yield on the 2170 assembly line was initially about 40%. Changing equipment conditions from that headline number alone could mistake a judgment error for a real defect. The reverse was equally risky: treating an actual process defect as an inspection issue would allow it to recur. Before changing the process, the production, inspection, retest, and scrap quantities needed one yield denominator.

My scope

As the assembly-process lead, I organized the production plan, WIP, defects, and downtime. I aligned the denominator and defect names used by production, quality, equipment, and development, coordinated the MSA review and condition-by-condition retests, and carried confirmed conditions into field standards.

Workflow

Align the yield denominator → separate judgment error from actual defects with MSA → check jigs, recipes, consumables, and inspection conditions → retest one condition at a time → update SOP, QCP, and P-FMEA

Key decisions and verification

  • I used MSA to review operator judgment error and measurement-system variation before classifying actual process defects.
  • Jigs, recipes, consumable wear, and inspection conditions were checked as one system, but only one condition changed in each trial.
  • The test item, inspection method, and decision rule stayed fixed. A condition entered the line baseline only after repeat results supported it.

Result and standardization

The team result recorded in career material was an improvement in good-product yield from about 40% to 98.7%. Confirmed conditions and checks were added to SOPs, QCP, P-FMEA, inspection criteria, and operator training. The practical standard was that different operators and shifts would calculate and judge yield on the same basis.

Transferability

When yield is low, verify the denominator and the measurement system before tuning a recipe. Then separate equipment, consumable, and inspection variables and retest one condition at a time so the cause and effect remain explainable.

Limits

The 98.7% figure is a career result for this line, verification period, and denominator, not a public-data recalculation or a universal result for every 2170 line. Company drawings, recipes, raw quality records, and customer specifications remain confidential.

Problem

Equipment-signal noise and real process variation were mixed together, while winding downtime and component-life issues repeatedly destabilized pilot yield.

Goal

Separate signal quality, equipment state, process conditions, and measurement behavior, then lock validated conditions into standards, QCP, P-FMEA, and MSA.

Success

Stabilize verified-scope 2170 good-product yield from about 40% to 98.7% and record accepted conditions in SOP, QCP, P-FMEA, and inspection criteria.

Scope and boundaries

In scope

  • Equipment-signal quality versus process-condition variation
  • Shared tracking of jig tolerances, recipes, wear parts, inspection conditions, and defects
  • IATF 16949-aligned standards, QCP, P-FMEA, and MSA

Out of scope or unverified

  • Company raw data, equipment drawings, recipes, and customer specifications
  • Claiming identical results across every cylindrical-cell line
  • Adding long-term Cpk or OEE without public evidence

Implementation and verification

What had to work

  • Separate signal quality from actual process values in the event timeline.
  • Classify stops, loss, and defects by equipment, process, material, and work condition.
  • Transfer validated conditions into QCP, P-FMEA, and standard work.

Quality and operating rules

  • Do not approve before/after comparisons when the measurement system changes.
  • Prefer stable repeated-lot ranges over a single best value.
  • Preserve confidentiality and metric boundaries in public wording.

Why it was designed this way

  1. Separate signal noise before tuning the process

    Treating a bad signal as a process shift can make condition changes increase variation.

  2. Check equipment state with process settings

    Jig tolerance and wear can look like a process-setting effect, so the physical state was checked in the same trial record.

  3. Lock improvements through document gates

    QCP, P-FMEA, MSA, and standard work—not individual memory—must define the next lot's baseline.
