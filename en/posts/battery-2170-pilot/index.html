    TECHNICAL CASE STUDY

    2170 Assembly-Line Good Yield: About 40%→98.7%

    A 2170 assembly-line stabilization case that used MSA to separate measurement misclassification from actual defects before changing jigs, recipes, wear parts, and inspection conditions

    Jul 31, 2026 · 5 minutes · Last reviewed Jul 31, 2026

    • 2170
    • Battery
    • Yield
    • MSA
    • SOP

    Problem and context

    Good-product yield on the 2170 assembly line was initially about 40%. Before increasing output, we had to distinguish whether losses came from the product, equipment, or inspection. Treating a gauge error as a process defect can lead to the wrong process change; treating a real defect as an inspection issue allows it to recur.

    I therefore started by aligning production quantity, inspected quantity, defect categories, downtime, and measurement rules rather than tuning one recipe from a headline yield number.

    What I owned

    • Managed the production plan, WIP, defect, and downtime view as the assembly-process lead.
    • Aligned the denominator and defect names used by production, quality, equipment, and development teams.
    • Reviewed MSA and Gage R&R with quality engineers to separate misclassification from actual process defects.
    • Tracked owners and retest dates for jig tolerances, recipes, wear parts, vision, and inspection conditions.
    • Applied confirmed conditions to SOPs, QCP, P-FMEA, inspection criteria, and operator training.

    What I checked and changed

    CheckWhyAction
    Yield denominator and defect namesPrevent departments from comparing different numbersPut production, inspection, retest, and scrap rules in one view
    Measurement systemAvoid counting misclassification as a process defectReviewed MSA/Gage R&R and separated inspection and process causes
    Jig and alignmentCheck tolerance and wear effects on positionRefined jig tolerance and replacement/inspection rules
    Recipes and wear partsKeep setting changes separate from physical wearChanged one variable at a time and retested under the same criteria
    Vision and inspectionMatch detection conditions to the actual CTQRechecked decision conditions against the quality rule

    The trial object, inspection method, and acceptance rule were held constant. A change reached the line only after a repeat check supported it.

    Result and limits

    • Good-product yield improved from about 40% to 98.7% within the verified scope.
    • The confirmed settings and checks were recorded so different operators and shifts could follow the same baseline.
    • I did not modify MES directly; I documented management-field and Cell Tracking requirements for the responsible system team.

    The 98.7% figure is a career result for this line, period, and denominator. It is not a third-party recalculation from public raw data or a universal result for every 2170 line. Company drawings, recipes, equipment addresses, customer specifications, and raw quality data remain private.

    Problem

    Equipment-signal noise and real process variation were mixed together, while winding downtime and component-life issues repeatedly destabilized pilot yield.

    Goal

    Separate signal quality, equipment state, process conditions, and measurement behavior, then lock validated conditions into standards, QCP, P-FMEA, and MSA.

    Success

    Stabilize verified-scope 2170 good-product yield from about 40% to 98.7% and record accepted conditions in SOP, QCP, P-FMEA, and inspection criteria.

    Scope and boundaries

    In scope

    • Equipment-signal quality versus process-condition variation
    • Shared tracking of jig tolerances, recipes, wear parts, inspection conditions, and defects
    • IATF 16949-aligned standards, QCP, P-FMEA, and MSA

    Out of scope or unverified

    • Company raw data, equipment drawings, recipes, and customer specifications
    • Claiming identical results across every cylindrical-cell line
    • Adding long-term Cpk or OEE without public evidence

    Implementation and verification

    What had to work

    • Separate signal quality from actual process values in the event timeline.
    • Classify stops, loss, and defects by equipment, process, material, and work condition.
    • Transfer validated conditions into QCP, P-FMEA, and standard work.

    Quality and operating rules

    • Do not approve before/after comparisons when the measurement system changes.
    • Prefer stable repeated-lot ranges over a single best value.
    • Preserve confidentiality and metric boundaries in public wording.

    Why it was designed this way

    1. Separate signal noise before tuning the process

      Treating a bad signal as a process shift can make condition changes increase variation.

    2. Check equipment state with process settings

      Jig tolerance and wear can look like a process-setting effect, so the physical state was checked in the same trial record.

    3. Lock improvements through document gates

      QCP, P-FMEA, MSA, and standard work—not individual memory—must define the next lot's baseline.
