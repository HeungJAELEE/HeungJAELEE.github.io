Vacuum-Robot Overhaul Standardization

Turning tacit work into a repeatable sequence for disassembly, assembly, alignment, leak testing, repeatability, and training

Project period
Feb 2021–Mar 2022
Context
Completed in Bontech's Robot Technology Team
Scope
Disassembly, cleaning, component replacement, precision assembly, final inspection, SOP, and training

Field problem and result summary

Field problem
Experience-dependent assembly and inspection created variation in work time and missed checks.
What I owned
While performing overhaul and precision assembly, I documented parts, torque, alignment, leak, and repeatability checks in run sheets and SOPs and trained operators.
Verified result
Rework and overtime were observed to decrease, while team monthly output increased from 1–2 to 4–5 units during the same period.

Problem and context

Vacuum-robot overhaul does not end with cleaning and parts replacement. Arm alignment, encoder zero, vacuum leak, and repeatability must be within the acceptance rule before the robot returns to equipment. Work order and inspection know-how had depended heavily on individual experience, leading to different assembly times and missed checks.

What I owned

I performed the sequence incoming check → ARM disassembly → body/base disassembly → cleaning and part replacement → body/base assembly → vacuum check → ARM assembly → straightness and repeatability → shipment → site installation and teaching. Fasteners were controlled with a torque wrench, position and alignment were checked with FARO equipment, encoder zero was set, and coordinates were checked with the teaching pendant. Helium leak, abnormal noise, and drive condition were also part of the final checks.

I separated the work into arm, center, and base units, then documented parts, fastening order, inspection methods, corrective actions, and replacement criteria in run sheets, SOPs, and checklists. The documents were used for operator training and first-job review.

Separating work variation with 4M

I did not treat overhaul variation as an operator-skill issue alone. A 4M review separated training needs from part, machine, and inspection-standard problems.

4MIssue reviewedStandardized item
ManVerbal training produced different sequences and missed checksTraining order, step ownership, and first-job review
MachineBearing, belt, and motor wear; arm alignment; encoder zero; leak; and abnormal noise had different causesSeparate part-condition, alignment/zero, leak, and repeatability checks
MaterialReplacement-part specifications, seal condition, cleanliness, and reuse condition affected assemblyParts list, replacement criteria, and incoming/cleaned-part records
MethodDisassembly and assembly order, torque, check location, and judgement depended on experienceFastening order, torque, inspection method, and abnormal-response steps in the SOP and checklist

This prevented assembly and inspection issues from being changed at the same time. Part condition was checked first, alignment and zero followed, and leak and repeatability confirmed the assembled result at the end.

Building the work standard with DMAIC

  1. Define — Reduce operator-dependent assembly time and missed checks while applying the same pre-shipment quality sequence.
  2. Measure — Record incoming condition, overhaul duration, replaced parts, torque, the 7 mm and 50 μm criteria, at least 15 repeat checks, and leak results by stage.
  3. Analyze — Use 4M to separate the effects of proficiency, part condition, assembly and alignment, and inspection method.
  4. Improve — Document arm, center, and base sequences, parts, torque, alignment, zero, and corrective actions and use them in training.
  5. Control — Retain work and final-inspection records and review the first job after training to confirm shop-floor use.

The documents were written so the next operator could follow the same assembly order and find an abnormality through the same tests. Two acceptance items were kept distinct: vertical end-effector movement at maximum travel had to remain within the customer’s 7 mm criterion, while lateral variation at the final stop had to remain within the 50 μm repeatability criterion. Repeatability was checked at least 15 times, and one overhaul generally took about 7–14 days.

Result and shop-floor use

  • Documented verbal know-how as SOPs, checklists, and training material and used them in actual work.
  • Rework and overtime were observed to decrease during the same period.
  • During the same period, team output increased from 1–2 to 4–5 units per month.
  • Connected assembly and inspection in one flow to reduce operator-dependent omissions.
  • At an overseas customer site, I also supported line setup, teaching, and the initial sample qualification completed during my employment.

The output figure is the team’s monthly shipment range during the same period and was also affected by proficiency, demand, material availability, and field operations. Rework and overtime were shop-floor observations rather than separately measured KPI results. My direct contribution was the overhaul work, inspection sequence, run-sheet/SOP preparation, and operator training.
