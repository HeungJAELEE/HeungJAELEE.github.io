TECHNICAL CASE STUDY

Production result

High-Temperature Anodizing Film Development for OLED Parts

Addressing crack and particle issues under repeated high-temperature use through sample scale-up and bath-control criteria

Project period
Development and production application during YMC employment
Context
Completed in YMC's Technical R&D Center

30 SECOND READ

30-second summary

Problem
Film cracking and particles recurred within warranty life under repeated high-temperature use.
My role
I redesigned film and process conditions, evaluated small, medium, and full-size samples, and established bath-control criteria.
Outcome / current stage
The revised condition entered production and was monitored for about a year through the transition period.
Team result
Production application and roughly one year of follow-up
My direct contribution
Process-condition redesign, staged sample evaluation, and bath-analysis criteria

Why the problem mattered

OLED process components are repeatedly exposed to high temperatures. A part may pass initial appearance and thickness checks yet develop film cracks and particles during use. That affects maintenance intervals and warranty life, not just cosmetic quality. The repeated field issue required process conditions, film thickness, bath control, and the inspection method to be reviewed together.

Company raw data and customer specifications cannot be disclosed. This case is therefore redrawn from career evidence and work I performed, with customer and product identifiers removed.

Initial state and evaluation frame

I did not treat “high-temperature cracking” as a single-process cause. Incoming surface state, pretreatment, film formation, thickness, bath condition, post-treatment, and customer use conditions could all affect the result.

Area Question
Incoming and pretreatment Were material and surface conditions comparable?
Film formation Did the condition and thickness fit repeated high-temperature use?
Bath analysis Could bath variation explain lot-to-lot differences?
Sample evaluation Would small-sample results survive scale-up?
Customer evaluation Did internal criteria match actual use?

Actions I performed

1. Narrowing the process window on small samples

Starting with full-size components would have made failures expensive and harder to diagnose. I compared film conditions and thicknesses on samples around 100×100 mm and reduced the number of candidates carried forward.

2. Checking uniformity while increasing sample size

Selected conditions were applied to samples around 500×500 mm. I reviewed positional variation rather than only an average result, then moved to near-full-size samples to check whether geometry and area introduced new differences.

3. Managing the condition and its analytical control together

A revised recipe is not repeatable if bath condition is not controlled by the same rules. Sampling, analysis frequency, and acceptance methods were therefore included in the work sequence. Detailed chemistry remains confidential; the important point is that the process change and the method used to maintain it were treated as one task.

4. Closing the loop with customer evaluation

An internal sample result was not considered final if the issue reappeared under customer use. Customer findings were brought back into process and inspection reviews until both sides showed the same direction.

Verification and retesting

The sequence was small sample → medium sample → near-full-size sample → customer evaluation → production application. Each scale introduced its own variation check.

Crack and particle issue in use
→ separate pretreatment, film, bath, and inspection factors
→ compare small-sample conditions
→ scale to medium and near-full-size samples
→ customer evaluation and revision
→ production application
→ follow the transition and recurrence status

Result and standardization

The revised condition entered production. After a transition period in which legacy-condition parts were still present, application status was followed for about a year. The outcome was recorded not only in the process recipe but also in bath-analysis criteria, sample-evaluation order, inspection method, and customer-review items.

The value of this case is not an isolated defect-rate figure. It is the sequence used to avoid treating one successful lab sample as production readiness.

Transferable principles

  1. Do not call a lab result production readiness.
  2. Treat variation introduced at each scale as a separate risk.
  3. Define the process condition and the analysis/inspection method that maintains it together.
  4. Do not close the case before customer-use evidence is consistent.
