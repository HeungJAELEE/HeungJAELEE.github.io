Manufacturing case study

Field application

High-Temperature Anodizing Film Development for OLED Parts

Addressing crack and particle issues under repeated high-temperature use through sample scale-up and bath-control criteria

Project period
Development and production application during YMC employment
Context
Completed in YMC's Technical R&D Center
Scope
Cause hypotheses, sample scale-up, analysis criteria, customer evaluation, and production documentation

Field problem and result summary

Field problem
Film cracking and particles recurred within warranty life under repeated high-temperature use.
What I owned
I redesigned film and process conditions, evaluated small, medium, and full-size samples, and established bath-control criteria.
Verified result
The revised condition entered production and was monitored for about a year through the transition period.

Why the problem mattered

OLED process components are repeatedly exposed to temperatures around 380–420°C. A part may pass initial appearance and thickness checks yet develop film cracks and particles during use. That affects maintenance intervals and warranty life, not just cosmetic quality. The repeated field issue required process conditions, film thickness, bath control, and the inspection method to be reviewed together.

Initial state and evaluation frame

I did not treat “high-temperature cracking” as a single-process cause. Incoming surface state, pretreatment, film formation, thickness, bath condition, post-treatment, and customer use conditions could all affect the result.

AreaQuestion
Incoming and pretreatmentWere material and surface conditions comparable?
Film formationDid the condition and thickness fit repeated high-temperature use?
Bath analysisCould bath variation explain lot-to-lot differences?
Sample evaluationWould small-sample results survive scale-up?
Customer evaluationDid internal criteria match actual use?

Separating the high-temperature film problem with 4M

Treating cracks and particles as a film-thickness problem alone could produce a good lab sample and an unstable production result. I used 4M to set the test sequence and control items.

4MIssue reviewedCheck direction
ManSampling location, titration endpoint, and appearance judgement could vary by analystAligned sampling location and frequency, analysis sequence, and inspection rules
MachineRectifier output, jig contact, bath temperature and agitation, and large-area position could affect film uniformityCompared film condition by sample size and position
MaterialIncoming surface and bath chemistry, including sulfuric acid, free sulfuric acid, oxalic acid, dissolved aluminium, and tartaric acid, could drive lot variationReviewed incoming condition and bath composition in the same trial record
MethodPretreatment, film condition and thickness, post-treatment, sampling, replenishment, and scale-up order affected the resultRetested in stages from small to near-full-size samples

Material and method could not be managed independently. The same recipe could produce a different film when bath chemistry changed, so process conditions and bath-analysis rules were controlled together.

Carrying the film development through DMAIC

  1. Define — Set cracks and particles during repeated 380–420°C use and warranty-period customer issues as the improvement target.
  2. Measure — Separated material, pretreatment, film, bath, and inspection conditions and recorded small-sample results and positional variation.
  3. Analyze — Used 4M to determine whether the issue came from the film condition alone or from bath chemistry, large-area uniformity, and inspection together.
  4. Improve — Narrowed candidates at about 100×100 mm, then moved through 500×500 mm and near-full-size samples while revising the condition and analysis rules.
  5. Control — Retained weekly sampling and titration, recipe and inspection rules, and customer-evaluation items and followed production application.

Actions I performed

Step 1 — Narrowing the process window on small samples

Starting with full-size components would have made failures expensive and harder to diagnose. I compared film conditions and thicknesses on samples around 100×100 mm and reduced the number of candidates carried forward.

Step 2 — Checking uniformity while increasing sample size

Selected conditions were applied to samples around 500×500 mm. I reviewed positional variation rather than only an average result, then moved to near-full-size samples to check whether geometry and area introduced new differences.

Step 3 — Managing the condition and its analytical control together

A revised recipe is not repeatable if bath condition is not controlled by the same rules. Sulfuric acid, free sulfuric acid, oxalic acid, dissolved aluminium, and tartaric acid were analyzed periodically. Weekly sampling and neutralization titration were used to review bath condition, while replenishment considered treated area and the following week’s production plan. The process change and the method used to maintain it were treated as one task.

Step 4 — Closing the loop with customer evaluation

An internal sample result was not considered final if the issue reappeared under customer use. Customer findings were brought back into process and inspection reviews until both sides showed the same direction.

Verification and retesting

The sequence was small sample → medium sample → near-full-size sample → customer evaluation → production application. Each scale introduced its own variation check.

  1. Confirm the crack and particle issue seen during customer use.
  2. Separate pretreatment, film, bath, and inspection factors.
  3. Compare chemistry and thickness conditions on small samples.
  4. Scale to medium and near-full-size samples and recheck repeatability.
  5. Use customer evaluation to revise conditions and inspection methods.
  6. Apply the selected condition in production and follow the transition and recurrence status.

Result and standardization

The revised condition entered production. After a transition period in which legacy-condition parts were still present, application status was followed for about a year. The outcome was recorded not only in the process recipe but also in bath-analysis criteria, sample-evaluation order, inspection method, and customer-review items.

The value of this case is not an isolated defect-rate figure. It is the sequence used to avoid treating one successful lab sample as production readiness.

What I carried into later manufacturing work

  1. Do not call a lab result production readiness.
  2. Treat variation introduced at each scale as a separate risk.
  3. Define the process condition and the analysis/inspection method that maintains it together.
  4. Do not close the case before customer-use evidence is consistent.
