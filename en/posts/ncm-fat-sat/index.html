The three-minute case

Why it mattered

An automatic cycle on one machine does not make a pilot line production-ready. Interfaces and acceptance criteria must work across the line, and unmet items must be retested against the same criteria after supplier action. The task was to manage 13 detailed processes and 11 core systems as one acceptance flow.

My scope

My scope covered acceptance criteria and FAT/SAT sheets, Punch List tracking, supplier actions and retest review, and daily 200-cell production operation. I connected equipment action status with material, WIP, and work priorities. Yield and delivery metrics are identified separately as team results.

Workflow

Define acceptance criteria → FAT/SAT → Punch List → supplier action → retest → daily 200-cell operation

Key decisions and verification

  • Acceptance criteria were defined before testing so the supplier and site team judged the same items.
  • A Punch List item was not closed on a planned action alone. I tracked the supplier action through a retest against the same criterion.
  • Production planning did not sit outside equipment acceptance. The daily 200-cell operation checked whether material, WIP, and work flow held together after FAT/SAT.

Result and standardization

The team recorded at least 95% pilot assembly yield and 100% delivery compliance. Acceptance criteria, Punch List and retest results, work standards, and production-history rules were connected so equipment acceptance led into daily operation on one baseline.

Transferability

Equipment acceptance is not a one-time FAT or SAT event. A line is ready only when acceptance criteria, open items, supplier actions, retests, and actual production operation remain traceable as one chain.

Limits

The yield and delivery figures are team results from pilot production, not long-term mass-production performance or a claim about each machine in isolation. Company acceptance records, equipment drawings, recipes, and customer specifications remain confidential.

Problem

Even when individual equipment runs, pilot defects remain hard to reproduce if line I/O, interlocks, measurement variation, welding conditions, and MES trace fields are disconnected.

Goal

Manage FAT/SAT for 11 core systems, process capability (Cpk), the measurement system (Gage R&R), welding/filling, and production planning in one acceptance flow.

Success

Retest equipment punches and operate a 200-cell daily plan to reach at least 95% pilot assembly yield and 100% on-time completion.

Scope and boundaries

In scope

  • FAT/SAT and punch closure for 11 core assembly systems
  • Process capability (Cpk), measurement system (Gage R&R), welding and filling
  • 200-cell daily plan, material, WIP, staffing, and lot history

Out of scope or unverified

  • Vendor drawings, customer specifications, raw recipes, and defect data
  • Generalizing pilot yield to long-term high-volume performance
  • Redistributing certification or customer approval documents

Implementation and verification

What had to work

  • Separate FAT and SAT criteria for I/O, safety, performance, recovery, and documents.
  • Trace measurement and welding by lot, equipment, recipe, and inspection result.
  • Include punches, standard work, training, and MES fields in acceptance.

Quality and operating rules

  • Review process capability and the measurement system separately; hold condition acceptance when measurement confidence is insufficient.
  • Check alarm, restart, and manual-recovery paths as well as normal cycles.
  • Separate pilot from production and team outcome from individual scope.

Why it was designed this way

  1. Separate FAT and SAT acceptance

    Standalone vendor conditions and on-site utilities and interfaces carry different risks.

  2. Separate Cpk and Gage R&R

    High measurement variation makes welding and defect cause conclusions unreliable.

  3. Include MES fields in equipment acceptance

    Without lot, recipe, and inspection linkage, post-startup root-cause analysis returns to manual work.
