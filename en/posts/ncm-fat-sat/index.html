Manufacturing case study

Pilot / validation

NCM Prismatic Line Acceptance and Early Stabilization Verified in Pilot Production

Taking 11 core machines and 13 main processes from FAT and SAT through actual cell production

Project period
Employment May 2023–May 2024 · FAT May–Jul 2023 · SAT Jul–Nov 2023
Context
Completed at Jastech, now JAS Robotics, in the Cell Development Team
Scope
Acceptance and retesting of 11 core machines, process checks across 13 operations, and pilot planning and traceability

Field problem and result summary

Field problem
Limited FAT samples and individual auto cycles could not prove end-to-end flow, repeatability, or readiness for actual production.
What I owned
I built acceptance sheets and punch lists, tracked supplier corrections and retests, and led actual cell production plus measurement and process-standard work.
Verified result
Initial assembly yield was about 95% across roughly 1,000 cells over five days, including reworked cells that met quality criteria.
Initial assembly yield About 95%
Acceptance scope 11 core machines · 13 main processes

Auto cycle alone was not enough for acceptance

A machine moving by itself is different from repeatedly producing actual cells. FAT with limited samples could confirm function and basic repeatability, but the installed line also had to align upstream and downstream flow, semi-finished-part tolerances, operator movement, and recovery after abnormalities.

I built acceptance sheets from the product drawings and process concept. They covered I/O and sensors, alarms and interlocks, precision and repeatability, cycle time, recipe storage, operator safety, and recovery behavior. The scope was 13 main production processes and 11 core machines; the EOL height-inspection unit was managed separately.

I did not classify every symptom as a machine fault. I divided candidates across work sequence and abnormal-response behavior, machine alignment and sealing, material and semi-finished tolerances, and recipe and inspection methods. The 4M view was used to narrow retest scope, not as a decorative report table.

FAT findings were retested in SAT and pilot production

Items checked in FAT were tested again after installation using the same criteria. Each issue was recorded in a punch list with the symptom, quality or safety impact, responsible supplier and department, due date, and retest method.

The operating sequence was straightforward:

FAT check → installation and SAT → punch registration → supplier or department correction → retest with the same criteria → sample and pilot production → acceptance or further action

I did not close an item merely because a supplier reported a correction. Actual cells were run to confirm that they could move to the next process and that results repeated under the same condition. When FAT and SAT differed, the products and measurements from the installed line took priority.

We traced cell thickness to narrow the swelling cause

Early cells showed abnormal thickness. During teardown after charge and discharge, we observed a yellow surface pattern suspected to be related to a side reaction. There was no normal-cell baseline at the time, so the appearance alone could not establish a cause.

We tracked thickness across electrode, J/R, before and after filling, pre-charge, seal pin, and formation. The increase became clear after pre-charge. Moisture control, Al₂O₃-coated separator, and electrolyte conditions were compared, but no meaningful difference explained the swelling, so they were removed from the leading candidates.

The remaining issue was insufficient gas release during pre-charge. We adjusted pre-charge and degassing vacuum pressure and pressing pressure, then built cells again. Teardown appearance, cell thickness, capacity, and life were checked together to confirm the direction. This was a swelling-analysis result and was not treated as the single cause of the line’s roughly 95% assembly yield.

Manufacturability was checked through a separate process validation and a component trial

Pre-forming: a shape criterion to reduce electrode damage

To reduce positive- and negative-electrode uncoated-edge damage and short risk before ultrasonic welding, manual shaping was replaced with a jig standard. Shape and position criteria were validated with samples and used in subsequent pilot production; the jig development, validation, and documentation are covered in a separate case.

C-cut: an allowable range for supplied components

To review a supplier’s cap-plate tooling proposal, the team built five cells for each of seven conditions, 35 cells in total. Can-to-cap-assy gap, incoming-part condition, laser penetration and beam ingress, beading geometry, and pressure stability were compared; I proposed an allowable range and drafted an IQC revision. Parts for the requested 2,000-unit follow-up run had not arrived before I left, so production adoption and cost effects were not verified.

Measurement methods became pilot operating criteria

Continuous values such as height, weight, and thickness require a trustworthy measurement system. In FAT, repeatability was checked with a master jig and reference samples. The same method became the pilot MSA criterion. The method itself did not change; the goal was consistent judgment across operators and time.

The confirmed methods and criteria were reflected in the QCP, internal process standards, and manufacturing checklists. After a process change, measurements were checked against the control range; out-of-range results returned to the cause candidates and punch items. Instead of exposing DMAIC as a table of contents, measurement validation, cause narrowing, action, retest, and standard revision were kept as one operating loop.

Initial production results and operating criteria were handed over

The team produced roughly 1,000 cells over about five days. Initial assembly yield was about 95%, including reworked cells that met quality criteria. I managed production priorities and material, WIP, and lot history, and reflected confirmed conditions in OPLs, SOPs, checklists, and the QCP.

One separate issue remained: process-level MES data was not connected at the cell level. I addressed it by building a cell-S/N production-history lookup tool.
