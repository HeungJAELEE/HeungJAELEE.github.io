Failure case

Diffuser Ultrasonic Stripping Scale-up Failure and Stop Gates

A failed scale-up in which chemical-use constraints and cavitation erosion prevented surface-quality and reuse gates from passing

Project period
National R&D project, Oct 2018–Dec 2019
Context
Completed in YMC's Technical R&D Center
Scope
Lab and full-part trials, surface-damage analysis, EHS constraint review, and stop-gate definition

Premise, decision, and lesson

Initial premise
The initial chemical-plus-ultrasound trial reduced stripping time, but removing the chemical without fume exhaust increased work time, manual effort, and surface damage.
What I checked
I ran lab and full-part trials, roughness, microscopy and SEM–EDS analysis, and documented overlap, relative-motion, and reusability risks.
Decision rule
Normal operation stopped because cavitation erosion, particles, and reuse failed; the case produced stop gates for future scale-up.

Project overview

ItemDescription
PeriodOct 2018–Dec 2019
TypeNational R&D project for diffuser quality improvement
PartRemoval of AlF-type residue from an aluminum diffuser for cleaning and reuse
Direct roleLab and full-part trials, condition-based samples and measurement, surface-damage analysis, and requests for further testing
GoalReduce process time and high-pressure-water work while maintaining particles, surface quality, and reusability
Final decisionStable scale-up operation stopped and the legacy process resumed

The legacy flow—bead blasting, chemical immersion, high-pressure/manual cleaning, and drying—took about three days. The project tested ultrasonic cavitation as a faster stripping method.

Why the eight-hour result was not yet success

Initial lab and limited full-part trials used the existing stripping chemical with ultrasound and confirmed removal in about eight hours. Removal, appearance, roughness, and particles were checked, but cumulative damage under long residence and repeated reuse was not closed.

The scale-up equipment required fume exhaust for the chemical. The need was raised, but the facility was not installed for cost reasons, so the process changed to water and ultrasound. Manual and high-pressure-water work increased, and the full flow took about one and a half days.

Cavitation erosion evidence

Damage repeated in overlapping ultrasonic zones. Visual inspection, metallurgical microscopy, SEM–EDS, and Ra/Rz/Ry checks showed pitting, increased roughness, surface loss, and color change. Longer residence increased cumulative damage, while particle and customer-reuse results deteriorated.

Tests changed product/transducer height and distance and examined methods to break up the ultrasonic field. A fixed field could move the damage location but could not prevent cumulative impact; relative movement of the transducer or product was required.

The equipment was fabricated and installed and showed initial performance, but surface damage accumulated under reuse. The issue became evident around the third reuse and the legacy process was resumed.

Stop gates carried forward

Future scale-up should not enter detailed equipment design or customer-performance trials until surface damage, particles and reuse, relative motion and overlap, repeated-sample accumulation, EHS facilities, and explicit stop/approval authority are all closed.

Ultrasonic stripping feasibility was confirmed, but this was not a production-success case. Exact frequency, power, distance, and chemical conditions are excluded because they remain active process knowledge.
