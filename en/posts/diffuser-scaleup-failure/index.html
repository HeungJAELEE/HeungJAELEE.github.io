Failure case

Diffuser Ultrasonic Stripping Scale-up Failure Analysis

A case showing what must be closed before equipment fabrication and when a scale-up should stop

Project period
National R&D project during YMC employment
Context
Completed in YMC's Technical R&D Center
Scope
Sample evaluation, risk documentation, scale-up review, and prevention-gate definition

Premise, decision, and lesson

Initial premise
Lab stripping was possible, but overlapping ultrasonic zones damaged the surface and left roughness, diffusion, and reusability risks unresolved.
What I checked
I documented sample and scale-up results and turned relative product/transducer movement, repeatability, and stopping criteria into pre-fabrication checks.
Decision rule
Normal operation was not achieved; the case established that stripping feasibility and product reusability must be judged separately.

Where the project broke down

The goal was to ultrasonically remove residue from a diffuser and leave it reusable. Lab work confirmed that stripping could occur, but roughness, diffusion, surface damage, and reusability risks remained open when equipment scale-up began.

Conditions that remained open

ConditionState at the timeNext-stage rule
Product qualityRoughness, diffusion, and surface-damage risk remainedRepeat within the controlled range
ReusabilityRe-entry to the customer process was unverifiedCheck actual use conditions
RepeatabilityInsufficient repeat-sample evidencePass repeated samples
Equipment conditionTransducer overlap, relative motion, and fixture conditions were incompleteFix conditions before detailed design
Measurement and stopPass, stop, and approval criteria were insufficientAgree measurement, stop, and authority in advance

Scale-up result and stop decision

  • Stripping feasibility was confirmed, but reusable-product success criteria were not closed.
  • Equipment was scaled while risks remained open, and the same damage pattern returned.
  • Repeatable normal operation was not achieved, so the project was not treated as a success.

Quantifying the failure review

Five gates were defined for future work: product quality, reusability, repeatability, equipment conditions, and measurement/stop criteria. Stripping feasibility was observed, but all five gates were not closed before scale-up. The result is therefore expressed through the stop point and the five-gate prevention rule rather than an invented improvement or production-success percentage.

Criteria reconstructed after failure

For future work, the order was defined as product quality → reusability → repeatability → equipment conditions → stop criteria, with no move into detailed equipment design while a gate remained open. These were prevention rules reconstructed after failure, not formal gates that existed at the time.
